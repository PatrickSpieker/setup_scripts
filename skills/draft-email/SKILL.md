# Draft Email

Write as a capable colleague speaking thoughtfully to another person: direct,
conversational, specific, and considerate. Apply this skill to every email-drafting
request without requiring an explicit invocation. Adapt to the recipient and
purpose; do not turn every email into a technical support request.

## Before drafting

Identify the reader, what they already know, and what they should understand or do
after reading. Use available conversation and source material. Ask only when a
missing fact materially affects the draft; do not invent facts, deadlines,
commitments, familiarity, or urgency. Drafting does not authorize sending.

## Principles

- **Wording and punctuation.** Always use "Thanks" instead of "Thank you."
  Never use em dashes in email subjects or bodies. Use a comma, colon,
  parentheses, or a separate sentence instead.
- **Purpose first.** Use a specific subject line for a new email and make the
  purpose clear immediately. A short contextual opening is fine when it helps
  the reader understand the request. Avoid long setup before revealing the ask.
- **Brief but complete.** Include enough context to answer without another round
  of clarification. Keep relevant dates, identifiers, exact statuses, and links;
  remove details that merely demonstrate research. Do not explain concepts the
  recipient already knows. No arbitrary word limit or forced one-screen rule.
- **Natural language.** Write sentences Patrick could say face-to-face. Prefer
  ordinary words, contractions, active verbs, and personal subjects such as
  "we" and "you." Keep sentences and paragraphs manageable without compressing
  the email into fragments or a specification. Retain precise technical terms
  when appropriate to the reader; remove bureaucratic language and buzzwords.
- **Precision and candor.** Use concrete facts instead of vague adjectives or
  inflated claims. Separate observations from assumptions and opinions. State
  uncertainty honestly and bad news plainly; do not bury it in euphemisms.
- **Easy to answer.** Ask clear, distinct questions. Number multiple questions
  when that helps the recipient respond point by point. Request guidance
  politely rather than prescribing steps when the recipient knows the process.
  Make the next action and responsible person clear when relevant; include a
  deadline only when supported by the request or context.
- **Courtesy without ceremony.** A simple greeting and "Thanks" often suffice.
  Match warmth and formality to the relationship. Skip stock pleasantries,
  flattery, repeated thanks, and automatic closing offers. Stop when the message
  is complete. Be firm but considerate in complaints, disagreements, and refusals;
  explain relevant reasons without blame, sarcasm, defensiveness, or condescension.
- **Light formatting.** Default to plain paragraphs with blank lines. Use lists
  for genuinely parallel questions or reference details. Avoid bold labels,
  section headings, and decorative emphasis in ordinary emails; use them only
  when length or complexity makes them useful. Keep supporting details after
  the main request when they would interrupt its flow.

## Edit before presenting

Review the draft from the recipient's perspective:

1. Is the purpose apparent, and is the requested response or next step clear?
2. Does the order make sense? Are there missing facts or ambiguous references
   that would force the recipient to ask for clarification?
3. Can anything be cut without losing meaning, useful context, or warmth?
4. Does it sound like Patrick speaking naturally to this particular reader?
5. Are names, dates, numbers, identifiers, links, quotations, and factual claims
   accurate against the supplied sources? Fix typos without copying mistakes
   from examples of Patrick's writing.

Return a ready-to-use draft, with a subject for new emails. Preserve the existing
subject for an ordinary reply. Avoid surrounding commentary or alternatives
unless requested or needed to flag a material unresolved fact.

## Basis

Patrick's edited AdvancedMD inquiry (November 2, 2026) establishes the personal
style: a brief purpose and context, natural questions, exact useful details,
light lists, and a simple thanks. Its incidental wording and support-request
structure are examples, not mandatory templates.

The writing principles also draw on Kenneth Roman and Joel Raphaelson,
*Writing That Works*, third edition (2000), especially chapters 2, 4, 5, 12,
and 13: write for action, respect the reader's time and knowledge, use natural
and precise language, make the response easy, and edit for logic, accuracy,
tone, and readability. This skill paraphrases those principles and is
self-contained; the book's dated technology and etiquette prescriptions are
not requirements.
